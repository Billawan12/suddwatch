The Crisis

South Sudan faces one of the world’s worst flood emergencies.

Greater Upper Nile — Jonglei, Unity, and Upper Nile states — sits at the heart of the Sudd, one of Africa’s largest wetlands. Record rainfall every year since 2019 has made seasonal flooding a permanent crisis. Hundreds of thousands of people are displaced with little or no warning.

Why the gap exists

Cloud cover blinds optical satellites

During the May–November rainy season, Greater Upper Nile sees over 90% cloud cover for weeks at a time. Traditional satellites cannot see through clouds. Flooding goes undetected until it is too late to act.

Manual analysis takes days

Even when imagery is available, traditional workflows require a human analyst to download, process, and distribute data — typically 3–5 days. SuddWatch compresses this to under 60 minutes through full automation.

Last-mile delivery is broken

Even when flood data exists, it rarely reaches those who need it most. Village chiefs in remote areas have no way to receive structured alerts. SuddWatch closes this gap with SMS — any handset, no internet needed.

How It Works

Five automated steps.
No human intervention.

From the moment a Sentinel-1 satellite passes over Greater Upper Nile to the moment an alert lands on a village chief’s phone — every step runs automatically, every day, without anyone pressing a button.

ESA Sentinel-1 transmits radar pulses straight through cloud cover and rain — the only satellite sensor reliable enough for South Sudan’s rainy season. All data is freely available via the Copernicus programme.
1 — Sentinel-1 passes overhead
Every six days, Sentinel-1 transmits radar pulses toward Earth. Unlike optical cameras, radar sees through cloud, rain, and darkness. SuddWatch polls the Copernicus Data Space API and downloads the latest scene automatically.
Cloud cover is not an obstacle. Night is not an obstacle.
2 — Raw data is preprocessed
The raw scene undergoes orbit correction, thermal noise removal, radiometric calibration, Lee speckle filtering, and terrain correction to 10m resolution — automated through ESA’s SNAP toolbox.
3 — Flood water is identified
Calm water reflects radar energy away from the satellite. A six-stage cascade — Otsu thresholding, GMM, change detection, TPI filter, exclusion masking, morphological cleaning — isolates flood pixels. A Random Forest classifier validates the result.
4 — Human impact is quantified
The flood boundary is intersected with WorldPop 2020 (13.1M population records), the OSM road network (194K features), 382 health facilities, and 7,712 village polygons. Every village receives a flood risk score from 0–100%.
5 — Alerts are dispatched
A concise SMS goes to village chiefs via Twilio — works on any handset. A structured HTML situation report reaches UN coordinators by email. Both go out simultaneously, automatically, and are logged in the dashboard.
Total pipeline: under 60 minutes, every pass. Fully automated

Under the Hood

Built on open data.
Deployable anywhere.

Every data source is free and open. Every tool is open-source. The system runs on a standard laptop with zero cloud subscription cost.

ESA Copernicus Sentinel-1

C-band SAR at 10m resolution, IW GRD mode. Downloaded via the Copernicus Data Space OData API with OAuth2 authentication. Free open access — no licensing cost whatsoever.

dataspace.copernicus.eu

WorldPop 2020 Population Grid

High-resolution population estimates at 100m grid cells covering South Sudan at 13.1M records. Used to estimate affected populations and score village-level flood risk from 0–100%.

worldpop.org

OpenStreetMap Road Network

194,291 road features and 382 health facilities from OpenStreetMap. Used to classify road accessibility after flooding and flag at-risk clinics, hospitals, and PHCs in real time.

openstreetmap.org

On the Ground
Time is the difference between
evacuation and displacement.

When floodwaters rise in the Sudd Basin, communities have hours — not days — to move. Every minute of delay costs lives, livelihoods, and access to healthcare. SuddWatch exists to give back those minutes.

What changes with early warning
Families evacuate safely
With an hour's warning, families can move livestock, food stores, and belongings before roads become impassable. Without it, they lose everything.
Aid reaches communities in time
Coordinators with advance notice can pre-position supplies and deploy teams before access routes close — not scramble for alternatives after.
Health facilities stay accessible
Knowing which roads are about to flood lets health teams reroute or transfer patients before clinics become islands — not after.
Response costs drop significantly
Early warning consistently reduces emergency response costs by 30–50% compared to reactive deployment — a critical saving for under-resourced humanitarian operations.
The Dashboard

A full operational picture
in one place.

Designed for humanitarian coordinators — not data scientists. Everything from live flood maps to GIS exports is three clicks away.

Interactive flood maps
OpenStreetMap base with clickable village pins by risk level, flood extent polygons, road accessibility overlays, and health facility markers — updated with every satellite pass.
Full event history
A searchable archive of every detection event this season. Filter by date, state, county, or severity. Each event expands to show full impact, timing, and alert delivery status.
Performance monitoring
Four-tab view: Pipeline Timing, Detection Quality, SLA Compliance, and Stage Heatmap — all from the live database. Instantly see if any stage is running slow.
Multi-format export
Export as GeoJSON, Shapefile, CSV, GeoTIFF, or PDF situation report via a three-step builder with live format preview. Compatible with QGIS and ArcGIS.
Live humanitarian intelligence
OCHA, IFRC, WFP, and FAO situation reports pulled automatically via the ReliefWeb API — humanitarian context alongside satellite data in one screen.
Dark & light modes
Full dark and light themes, switchable from the sidebar. A plain-language glossary explains every technical term for non-specialist users.
Who Uses SuddWatch

Built for every level
of the response chain.

From village chiefs receiving a 160-character SMS to GIS analysts downloading GeoTIFF exports — SuddWatch serves every role in the humanitarian response.

Field Level
Village chiefs & community leaders
Receive a plain-language SMS the moment flooding is detected near their community. No smartphone, no internet, no app — works on any basic handset in the most remote locations.
Coordination Level
UN OCHA & IFRC coordinators
Receive a structured HTML situation report with flood extent, affected population, inaccessible roads, and at-risk health facilities — formatted for immediate distribution to response teams.
Technical Level
REACH & UNOSAT GIS analysts
Download GeoJSON, Shapefile, or GeoTIFF exports from the dashboard for use in QGIS and ArcGIS. Full raster flood mask for integration into broader mapping workflows.
